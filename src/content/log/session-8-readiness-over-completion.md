I arrived at the event just after 7:30am. The Training Officers Consortium 2026 Summit was underway and I scored a ticket thanks to a professional hero of mine. I checked into the event, I checked out the event partner kiosks, I checked in with my host for the day. Breakfast was served, so I put together a little plate. I had a few goals on the day and they started the moment I walked in. Every session that day created a lens through which to view the rapidly evolving world of work, and an opportunity to better understand the Learning and Development space at the State and Federal levels. The people working tirelessly across sectors to build partnerships, identify complex organizational problems, and provide lasting learning solutions were in the room and on stage. Session after session, they argued that completion is not a reliable measure of training, while readiness is.

I looked at my inbox and read an email about the upcoming program launch date for my partner and I couldn't help but think that my partner is in the business of completion rates, and by extension, so am I. So much of what I learned in my learning design career can be characterized by helping people to move from Point A to Point B in a learning experience; from untrained to trained. But more importantly, the speakers at the event argued, learners need to be prepared for what training cannot provide. They need the kind of training that prepares them for scenarios that no one has ever experienced. It seems both obvious and impossible at the same time, but that's what made it so valuable as a thought exercise. The summit spent the rest of the day unpacking that complexity.

To summarize some of the best points of the day:

- Content is the foundation, not the destination.
- Confidence without readiness is not completion.
- Trying to solve every problem is how L&D loses credibility.
- Talk to the people before you build.
- Don't overlook the anecdotes in the middle of the data.
- Resilience does not mean doing the same or more with fewer people - that's a burnout machine.

I heard the room discuss common organizational challenges. But the only solutions that resonated with the audience started and ended with people. No AI-as-an-end anywhere.

An overarching thought - While the job market is discouraging, broadly speaking, the federal contractor-space is thriving. Despite the fact that public-sector and contractor teams are building inside a permission structure that is shifting beneath them, the demand is there because of the sheer volume of learning and development problems. So at a time when people are generally repulsed by government and politics, people seeking government contracts should likely be running toward the government, rather than away from it.

Lastly, a funny story - I asked just two brief questions of two different panels in the morning:

- Since you and your teams adopted more AI, what have each of you stopped doing? And has anyone noticed?
- Given the topic of building credibility and trust, do you think that AI is pushing us closer together or further apart?

The speaker holding the stage after lunch referred to me as "AI George" in the context of his talk. He got a good laugh out of me and the whole room. But something very unique happened. People suddenly associated me with AI. People complimented me for asking AI-related questions. And they often asked me to weigh in on AI issues their organizations were facing. I thanked the speaker for the free branding at the end of his session and played my part for the rest of the day - surveying learning engineering problems.
